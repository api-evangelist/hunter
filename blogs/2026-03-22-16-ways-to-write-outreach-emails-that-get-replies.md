If you pick Google and stick with it, you will get good results across most audiences.</p><p>For manual email volume on a brand-new domain: start with two emails on day one, then add two more each day until you reach about 15 per day. </p><p>Hold at 15 for one to two weeks. Does using HTML signatures or images hurt deliverability on day one?</strong></h3><p><strong>Yes. HTML emails (including designed signatures with images and links) receive 674% more bounces than plain text emails, according to Hunter&apos;s data.</strong></p><p>HTML elements, including graphic signatures, hyperlinks, images, bold/italic formatting, and GIFs, trigger spam filters at  higher rates than plain text.</p><p>For outreach, the simpler the email, the better. Email providers run compliance and cybersecurity filters that flag links from unknown senders. If your email contains a link, it might not even reach the inbox.</p><p>On day one of a new domain, this is especially critical. Your accounts have no reputation yet. Adding HTML elements increases the chance that your emails land in spam before anyone ever sees them. It&apos;s also what makes it important to have ramp ups.</p><p>What to remove from your outreach emails:</p><ul><li>Hyperlinks (including in signatures)</li><li>Attachments</li><li>Graphics and images</li><li>Bold, italic, and underlined text</li><li>HTML-designed signature blocks</li></ul><p>Once you have a positive reply and a relationship is forming, you can switch to your main email with a full signature for ongoing conversation.</p><h3 id="7-whats-your-feedbackbest-practice-on-including-sender-signature-boxcontact-info"><strong>7. What&apos;s your feedback/best practice on including sender signature box/contact info?</strong></h3><p><strong>Keep your signature to plain text only: your name, title, company, location, and phone number.</strong></p><p>Oliver shared his exact signature format in the session: &quot;Oliver, CEO, Ascenxion, Cleveland, Ohio&quot; followed by his phone number. No links, graphics, logo, or social handles.</p><p>Make sure whatever is in your signature aligns closely with the domain you are sending from. </p><p>If your email comes from huntergrowth.io but your signature says &quot;Acme Corp,&quot; that disconnect makes the email look even more suspicious. </p><p>The brand name in your signature and the domain in your email address should clearly be the same company.</p><p>The full signature with links, logo, and social icons can come later, once you have moved the conversation to your primary email address after a positive response.</p><h3 id="8-how-do-you-offer-up-free-resources-without-putting-a-link-in-your-message"><strong>8. How do you offer up free resources without putting a link in your message?</strong></h3><p><strong>Describe the resource in your initial email and offer to send it, then include the link only after the recipient replies.</strong></p><p>Do not put links in the initial outreach email because that email is being sent at scale. If you send 100 emails with a link and get no responses, email providers may flag that link as suspicious.</p><p>Instead, create curiosity. Mention the resource, describe the outcome it delivers, and ask if they want it. </p><p>For example: &quot;We built a calculator that shows how much time your team spends on manual accounting each week. Want me to send it over?&quot;</p><p>When they reply &quot;yes,&quot; you are now in a one-to-one conversation. The email provider sees that the recipient engaged. You can safely include the link in your follow-up, and it is far less likely to be flagged. The volume is lower, there is an established thread, and there is demonstrated interest.</p><p>This approach also doubles as a qualification step. If someone replies asking for your resource, you know they have at least some level of interest. That is more valuable than a blind click on a link.</p><p></p><h2 id="content-and-messaging">Content and messaging</h2><h3 id="9-does-leading-with-value-mean-leading-with-outcomes-like-improve-your-roi-decrease-costs-improve-health-outcomes-and-how-specific-should-those-outcomesvalue-statements-be"><strong>9. Does leading with value mean leading with outcomes, like &quot;improve your ROI, decrease costs, improve health outcomes&quot;? And how specific should those outcomes/value statements be?</strong></h3><p><strong>Leading with value has two meanings: giving something tangible (like a resource or invitation) or, in a direct email, leading with a specific, pain-driven outcome.</strong></p><p>The first version of &quot;leading with value&quot; is literally giving the recipient something: an invitation to a podcast, a custom calculator, a research report, a pilot offer. The value they receive is tangible and separate from your pitch.</p><p>The second version applies to direct outreach. If you are writing a straightforward email about your service, then yes, lead with the outcome. But be specific, and lead with pain before the outcome.</p><p>Generic outcomes (&quot;improve your ROI&quot;) sound like every other email in their inbox. Specific, pain-driven outcomes cut through:</p><ul><li>Generic: &quot;We help companies improve their sales process&quot;</li><li>Specific: &quot;SaaS founders we work with were spending 10+ hours a week on manual accounting. We cut that to under two.&quot;</li></ul><p>The pain point is what makes your product or service unique. Outcomes, taken to their logical end, all sound the same (more revenue, lower costs). </p><p>The specific pain you solve is what differentiates you and what creates an emotional connection with the 97% of prospects who are not actively buying right now.</p><h3 id="10-do-you-find-a-one-line-qualifying-intro-helpful-up-front-eg-i-work-with-your-industry-peers-like-x-and-x-or-better-to-dive-into-whats-valuable-to-your-target-prospect"><strong>10. Do you find a one-line qualifying intro helpful up front (e.g., &quot;I work with your industry peers like X and X&quot;) or better to dive into what&apos;s valuable to your target prospect?</strong></h3><p><strong>Lead with value to the prospect first. Social proof can support your message, but it should not be the opening line.</strong></p><p>Oliver shared a framework for structuring outreach emails that is worth writing down:</p><ul><li>Open with a call-out (the outcome you help them achieve)</li><li>Follow with your offer (how you do it)</li><li>Add a testimonial, and then </li><li>Address the pain point</li></ul><p>But note, in some markets, name-dropping recognizable clients in the first line might immediately grab attention. In others, it&apos;s posturing. </p><p>The only  way to know is to test: send one version of 50 emails with the social proof up front and another 50 leading with value, and compare response rates.</p><p>Do not bury your value in the third paragraph. Whatever your opening approach, get to the point in the first sentence. If someone has to read for two minutes before understanding why you are emailing, you have already lost them.</p><h3 id="11-if-you-receive-a-reply-from-a-first-outreach-yes-please-send-more-info-and-send-more-info-and-then-you-dont-get-any-more-feedback-whats-the-best-way-to-react-to-that"><strong>11. If you receive a reply from a first outreach, &quot;yes, please send more info,&quot; and send more info and then you don&apos;t get any more feedback. What&apos;s the best way to react to that?</strong></h3><p><strong>If it is a one-off, do not overthink it. If it is happening repeatedly, the problem is in what you are sending, not in the prospect.</strong></p><p>If one person goes quiet after requesting more info, that is normal. People are busy, they&apos;re not rude. It&apos;s very easy to not respond to an email, and it does not necessarily mean the information was bad.</p><p>If the pattern keeps repeating, though, that is a signal. Evaluate what you are sending. Maybe the &quot;more info&quot; you provide is too generic, too long, or does not match what they expected. Consider these alternatives:</p><ul><li>Instead of sending a document, try pushing for a short call so you can tailor the information to their specific situation</li><li>Follow up with a genuine, low-pressure message: &quot;I hope that was useful. Did it hit the mark, or should I recalibrate?&quot;</li><li>Share something more specific, like a case study or data point relevant to their industry</li></ul><p>Matt emphasized that most people are willing to give you feedback if you ask politely and genuinely. They are very unlikely to respond with something harsh. But you have to ask, because they have no obligation to volunteer that feedback unprompted.</p><h3 id="12-3-4-follow-ups-is-best-if-you-dont-have-any-reply-but-what-should-i-do-if-an-email-is-opened-but-no-reply"><strong>12. 3-4 follow-ups is best if you don&apos;t have any reply, but what should I do if an email is opened but no reply?</strong></h3><p><strong>Treat an open without a reply the same way you would treat no response at all, and be cautious about trusting open data.</strong></p><p>Open tracking is inherently unreliable.</p><p>Open tracking works by embedding a tiny pixel in the email, and email providers do not always load that pixel accurately. </p><p>Some emails show as &quot;opened&quot; when they were only previewed in a notification. Others are opened but the pixel never fires. </p><p>Hunter&apos;s data shows that turning off open tracking can increase reply rates to 7.4%, because open tracking itself adds HTML elements that can hurt deliverability.</p><p>The solution isn&apos;t to change how you follow up based on if someone opens or not - you need to improve your emails</p><ul><li>Make the value clearer in the first sentence</li><li>Ensure your CTA is low-pressure (&quot;Can I send more info?&quot; not &quot;Let&apos;s book a call Tuesday&quot;)</li><li>Keep the email short. If everyone else is writing long emails, write shorter.</li></ul><p>Using three follow-up emails increases reply rates from 3.3% to 6.8%, according to Hunter&apos;s data. </p><figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-card-hascaption"><img alt="16 ways to write outreach emails that get replies" class="kg-image" height="974" src="https://hunter.io/blog/content/images/2026/03/image-5.png" width="2000" /><figcaption><span style="white-space: pre-wrap;">Adding multiple follow ups can lead to big gains</span></figcaption></figure><p>Stick to that cadence. Each follow-up should share new content or ask a new question, not just bump the thread.</p><h2 id="strategy-and-market">Strategy and market</h2><h3 id="13-does-any-of-this-advice-change-based-on-the-size-of-an-organization-for-example-a-solo-entrepreneur-doing-everything-themselves"><strong>13. Does any of this advice change based on the size of an organization, for example a solo entrepreneur doing everything themselves?</strong></h3><p><strong>No. The principles are the same whether you are a solo founder or a 100-person sales team.</strong></p><p>If you can build this system as a one-person business, you can build it at any scale. The infrastructure setup, the warmup process, the creative approach: none of it changes based on company size.</p><p>What does change is emphasis. A solo entrepreneur probably does not need 30 email accounts and 10 domains. The scalability side matters less when you are one person. What matters more is automating good messages so you can focus your time on conversations, not on manual sending.</p><h3 id="14-does-it-make-sense-to-post-on-as-many-social-media-platforms-as-possible-to-get-our-brand-out-there"><strong>14. Does it make sense to post on as many social media platforms as possible to get our brand out there?</strong></h3><p><strong>Social visibility helps, but it is not a requirement for successful outreach, and it depends heavily on your industry.</strong></p><p>Oliver&apos;s analogy from the session says it all: if someone knocks on your front door and you do not know who they are, you are skeptical. If you have seen them around the neighborhood, you are more likely to open the door. Social media presence works the same way. It builds familiarity.</p><p>Matt offered a suggestion- one of the most effective second touches he has used is not social media at all - it&apos;s retargeting ads. </p><p>A prospect receives an outreach email, visits the website, and then sees retargeting ads as they browse. </p><p>By the time the follow-up email arrives, they have seen the brand seven or eight times. </p><p>That familiarity increases response rates without requiring a prolific social media presence:</p><ul><li>If your prospects are active on social media (marketers, founders, tech companies), a LinkedIn presence adds credibility and familiarity</li><li>If your prospects are in industries that do not check social media (accounting, manufacturing, healthcare), it will not make or break your campaign</li><li>A good email is a good email regardless of your social footprint. Social presence supports outreach but does not replace strong messaging.</li></ul><h3 id="15-automation-has-exploded-over-the-past-couple-of-years-and-my-reply-rates-have-dropped-hiring-managers-say-theyre-getting-40-50-emails-a-day-so-they-ignore-most-is-genuine-outreach-being-drowned-out-by-automation"><strong>15. Automation has exploded over the past couple of years and my reply rates have dropped. Hiring managers say they&apos;re getting 40-50 emails a day, so they ignore most. Is genuine outreach being drowned out by automation?</strong></h3><p><strong>The volume of bad outreach has increased, but that is making it easier, not harder, for genuine outreach to stand out.</strong></p><p>This was a central theme of the session because 58% of decision makers still receive at least one valuable outreach email per month. That means the channel is working for senders who do it well, but most aren&apos;t doing outreach well.</p><p>The hiring manager receiving 40 to 50 emails a day is not ignoring all of them. </p><p>They are ignoring the ones that feel like AI slop (69%), the ones that are too pushy (65%), and the ones that are irrelevant (61%). A relevant, well-researched, low-pressure email still gets read.</p><p>Only 3% of your prospects are actively looking for a solution right now. If you focus only on that 3% with a direct pitch, you are competing with everyone else doing the same thing. </p><p>But if you take a relationship-driven approach (podcast invitations, research calls, sharing tools and resources), you can access up to 70% of the market. That is where the opportunity is, and it is exactly the kind of outreach that automation-heavy senders are not doing.</p><p></p><h2 id="performance-and-benchmarks">Performance and benchmarks</h2><h3 id="16-what-are-the-open-rates-by-use-case-eg-marketing-seo-sales-pr"><strong>16. What are the open rates by use case (e.g., marketing, SEO, sales, PR)?</strong></h3><p><strong>The overall average open rate across 31 million outreach emails is 30%, but reply rates vary dramatically by use case, which is the metric that actually matters.</strong></p><p>Open rates are useful for testing subject lines, but they are not the metric you should be optimizing for. Reply rates tell you whether your email actually compelled someone to act. Hunter&apos;s data breaks down reply rates by use case:</p><ul><li>Traditional sales outreach (asking for a meeting): 3%</li><li>Marketing campaigns to MQLs or existing accounts: 6.2%</li><li>Headhunting and recruiting: 7.5%</li><li>Digital PR: 13%</li></ul><p>The gap between a 3% sales reply rate and a 13% digital PR reply rate is not about the subject line. It is about the value exchange. Digital PR emails offer clear, mutual value (coverage, backlinks, quotes). Sales emails often ask without giving.</p><p>Open rates are less useful to compare by use case because they are heavily influenced by subject line, sender name, and sending time, all of which vary independently of the email&apos;s content. Focus on reply rates. That is the metric that predicts pipeline.</p><h3 id="id"></h3><h2 id="watch-the-replay">Watch the replay</h2><p>There&apos;s plenty more to uncover about email writing.
